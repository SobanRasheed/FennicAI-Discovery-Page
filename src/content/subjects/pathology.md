## How this subject is structured

Pathology moves from general principles to organs. **Cell Injury,
Inflammation & Repair** is the foundation: how cells are injured and die, how
the body responds with acute and chronic inflammation, and how wounds heal.
**Hemodynamic Disorders** covers what happens when blood flow and fluid
balance fail — oedema, thrombosis, embolism, infarction, and shock.
**Neoplasia** explains tumour biology: the difference between benign and
malignant growth, how cancers arise and spread, and how they are named.
**Systemic Pathology** then applies all of this organ by organ, from
atherosclerosis in vessels to glomerular disease in the kidney.

## How to study it

Study Pathology as mechanisms, not lists. For every disease, aim to connect
three layers: the cause, the morphological change it produces, and the
clinical sign that change explains. If you know why myocardial infarcts are
coagulative necrosis, you can reason to the scarred, thinned ventricular wall
and the raised troponin — one mechanism, three exam answers. Our
[cell injury study note](/notes/cell-injury/) models this approach, and the
[Inflammation and Repair topic page](/topics/inflammation-and-repair/)
collects the related concepts in one place.

Use the general-pathology chapters as an investment: time spent properly
understanding inflammation, thrombosis, and neoplasia pays off in every
systemic chapter afterwards, because systemic disease is general pathology
recurring in specific organs.

Finally, revise with applied questions rather than rereading. Work through
[the Pathology quiz](/practice/quiz/pathology/) regularly — and before the
written paper, review the common MCQ traps, because most lost marks in
Pathology come from reasoning errors rather than unknown facts.
