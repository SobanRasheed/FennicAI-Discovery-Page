## How this subject is structured

The chapters move from persons to duties. **Identification** covers how an
unknown body or living person is identified — features, fingerprints, dental
records, DNA — and the medicolegal estimation of age. **Injuries** classifies
wounds and their causation, from blunt and sharp force to burns and firearm
injuries, and teaches the difference between wounds sustained before and
after death. **Asphyxia** deals with deaths from interference with
respiration: hanging, strangulation, suffocation, and drowning. **Toxicology**
covers the general management of the poisoned patient and the important
poisons. **Medical Jurisprudence & Ethics** completes the subject with the
doctor's legal duties: certificates, records, consent, negligence, and court
evidence.

## How to study it

Study Forensic Medicine as lists of defined criteria, because that is how it
is examined. Learn the classic findings precisely — the ligature mark of
hanging, the signs of asphyxia, the duration ranges of rigor mortis — since
marks are awarded for the specific features, not for general clinical
impressions. Our [asphyxia study note](/notes/asphyxia/) shows the level of
precision to aim for on one of the subject's core topics.

For every topic, add the legal dimension: not just what the finding is, but
what a doctor must do upon finding it — which certificate to issue, what to
record, when to inform the police. Examiners consistently reward the legal
duty stated alongside the medical fact.

Finally, practise writing short, complete answers on the recurring
medico-legal documents, and test yourself with
[the Forensic Medicine quiz](/practice/quiz/forensic-medicine/) so the
criteria-based recall stays exact.
