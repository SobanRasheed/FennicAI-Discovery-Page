## How this subject is structured

The chapters follow the organism kingdoms. **General Bacteriology** covers
bacterial structure, staining behaviour, growth requirements, sterilisation,
and how the laboratory approaches an unknown organism. **Systemic
Bacteriology** then works through the pathogens: Gram-positive and
Gram-negative cocci, the bacilli, mycobacteria, and spirochaetes, organised
around the syndromes they cause. **Virology** covers replication strategies
and the major viral families, from hepatitis to influenza to HIV. **Mycology**
deals with the fungi, and **Parasitology** with the protozoa and helminths
that remain a major part of the disease burden in the region.

## How to study it

Anchor every organism to four facts: its appearance (stain and morphology),
its transmission, its typical disease, and its treatment. That quartet
answers most exam questions about any pathogen, and it is the information a
clinician actually retrieves when a culture report arrives.

For laboratory methods, learn each technique by what it distinguishes: the
Gram stain separates bacteria by wall structure, the Ziehl-Neelsen stain
identifies acid-fast organisms, and each culture medium selects for a
particular group. Understanding what a test is *for* beats memorising its
procedure step by step. Our note comparing
[Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria](/notes/gram-positive-vs-gram-negative/)
shows how deep a single organising distinction can go, and the
[Antimicrobial Resistance topic page](/topics/antimicrobial-resistance/)
covers the resistance mechanisms that connect this subject to
[Pharmacology](/subjects/pharmacology/).

Finally, revise with questions. [The Microbiology quiz](/practice/quiz/microbiology/)
drills the organism-by-organism distinctions that exams live on.
