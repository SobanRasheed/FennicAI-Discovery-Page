      No. Reading and testing should interleave from the start. Attempting

Pathology MCQ papers are generous to prepared students and merciless to
prepared students who answer badly. The difference is usually not
knowledge but exam behavior. These are the seven mistakes that cost the
most marks, with the wrong instinct behind each, why it fails, and the
fix that recovers the marks.

## 1. Buzzword Pattern-Matching Without Reading the Stem

The wrong instinct: "caseating granulomas, I know this one," and the
answer is marked before the third line of the stem is read. Why it
fails: examiners deliberately plant buzzwords alongside a detail that
changes the answer, an immune status, a duration, a location, an age.
The stem that says "caseating granuloma in a 45-year-old, sputum
positive" and the one that says "caseating granuloma in a
corticosteroid-treated patient" can have different best answers. The
fix: read the last sentence of the stem first, so you know exactly what
is being asked, then read the entire stem, then answer.

## 2. Confusing Necrosis Types by Location

The wrong instinct: matching a necrosis type to an organ from memory of
a list, without thinking about the mechanism. Why it fails: the
associations only stick when you know why. Coagulative necrosis is the
default in ischemic infarcts of solid organs because the architecture is
ghost-preserved, except the brain, where liquefactive necrosis dominates
because of the high lipid content and enzymatic environment. Caseous
necrosis means mycobacterial or fungal infection, a semi-solid
debris behind a granuloma, as covered in the
[cell injury note](/notes/cell-injury/). Fat necrosis follows pancreatic
enzyme release, and fibrinoid necrosis marks immune-mediated vessel
damage. The fix: learn each type with its mechanism, and each mechanism
with its one-line why. The location list then becomes impossible to
forget.

## 3. Missing "Most Common" Versus "Most Deadly"

The wrong instinct: answering the disease that left the strongest
impression. Why it fails: "most common" and "most often fatal" are
different questions with different answers, and examiners set both in
the same paper precisely to catch this. Osteosarcoma and osteochondroma
habitually swap places this way, as do papillary and anaplastic thyroid
carcinoma, the first common and indolent, the second rare and lethal.
The fix: circle the qualifier in the stem before answering. Underline
"most common", "most deadly", "most likely", "least likely" every time,
and build separate lists for each qualifier when you revise.

## 4. Skipping the "Except", "Least", and "Not" Qualifiers

The wrong instinct: reading four true statements and marking the first
one. Why it fails: the question asks for the false statement, and the
first true option is a deliberate trap for exactly this reading style.
The fix: same habit as above, but stricter. Any negative qualifier gets
underlined twice. If your university's papers allow rough work, write a
minus sign next to negative stems before looking at options.

## 5. Choosing the Diagnosis Before Reading All Options

The wrong instinct: seeing the correct diagnosis at option B and marking
it without reading C and D. Why it fails: one-best-answer papers
sometimes list two correct-sounding options where only one matches the
specific question asked, for example the finding that best explains a
laboratory value versus the most likely diagnosis. The fix: read every
option. It costs two seconds each and recovers marks every paper.

## 6. Revising by Rereading, Not Retrieving

The wrong instinct: "I will do questions after I finish the syllabus."
Why it fails: rereading inflates recognition, which feels like mastery
and collapses in the hall, where the task is retrieval under time
pressure. The fix: after every topic, attempt MCQs on it immediately,
even with notes half-finished, and use every wrong answer as a pointer
back to the relevant section of Robbins. The wrong answers are your
syllabus.

## 7. Guessing Without Eliminating

The wrong instinct: leaving a difficult question blank, or picking by
gut feel. Why it fails: negative marking or not, a considered guess
after eliminating two options beats an unconsidered one. The fix: for
every hard stem, eliminate the options you can disprove, then choose
between what remains. Mark the question for review and move on, so one
hard stem never eats four easy ones.

The pattern behind all seven is the same: the exam tests careful
production under constraint, not familiarity. Practice under timed
conditions with the [pathology quiz](/practice/quiz/pathology/), review
every option of every question, and the technique becomes as automatic
as the knowledge.
