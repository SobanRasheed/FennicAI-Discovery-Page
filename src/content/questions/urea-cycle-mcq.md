  - Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, activated by N-acetylglutamate
  - Ornithine transcarbamylase, activated by ornithine
  - Argininosuccinate synthetase, activated by citrulline
  - Arginase, activated by aspartate
answer: Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, activated by N-acetylglutamate
explanation: >
  Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I sits in the mitochondrial matrix,
  condenses ammonia with bicarbonate using two ATP, and is switched on by
  N-acetylglutamate, whose own synthesis rises after a protein meal — the
  classic feed-forward control that matches urea production to the
  nitrogen load. The key distractor, ornithine transcarbamylase, is the
  second mitochondrial step and its deficiency is the most common
  inherited urea-cycle defect, but it is not rate-limiting; arginase
  merely hydrolyses arginine in the final step.

## Question

Anchor the cycle spatially as well as enzymatically: the first two steps
occur in mitochondria, the remaining three in the cytosol, and the whole
pathway disposes of two nitrogen atoms — one from free ammonia and one
from aspartate — at the cost of four high-energy phosphate bonds. When
the cycle fails, hyperammonaemia with respiratory alkalosis and cerebral
oedema follows, which is why this enzyme pair appears so often in exams.
