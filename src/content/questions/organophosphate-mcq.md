  Pralidoxime can reactivate acetylcholinesterase after organophosphate
  poisoning, but only if it is given before "aging" occurs. What is aging?

options:
  - Loss of an alkyl group from the phosphorylated enzyme, which leaves a negatively charged residue that pralidoxime cannot displace
  - Hepatic metabolism of pralidoxime into an inactive glucuronide conjugate
  - Internalisation and lysosomal degradation of the inhibited enzyme molecule
  - Regeneration of fresh acetylcholinesterase by the liver, which removes the need for reactivation
answer: Loss of an alkyl group from the phosphorylated enzyme, which leaves a negatively charged residue that pralidoxime cannot displace
explanation: >
  The organophosphate first binds the active-site serine reversibly;
  pralidoxime can still nucleophilically attack and pull the phosphate
  off at this stage. With time, one alkyl group spontaneously cleaves from
  the bound phosphate — aging — leaving a strongly negatively charged
  phosphyl-enzyme that repels the similarly charged pralidoxime, making
  the inhibition permanent. The key distractor blames hepatic metabolism
  of pralidoxime, but aging is a change in the enzyme, not in the
  antidote.

## Question

Clinically this defines the treatment window: pralidoxime works best when
given early, alongside generous atropine for the muscarinic symptoms
(salivation, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia), which atropine controls whether
or not the enzyme has aged. The time to aging varies by agent — minutes
for some nerve agents, hours for many insecticides. Revise receptor
fundamentals on the [autonomic pharmacology note](/notes/autonomic-pharmacology/).
