options:
  - The thick peptidoglycan layer of gram-positive cells retains the crystal violet–iodine complex during decolorization
  - Gram-positive cells take up safranin more avidly than they take up crystal violet

  - Teichoic acids of gram-positive cells chemically bind the pink counterstain
answer: The thick peptidoglycan layer of gram-positive cells retains the crystal violet–iodine complex during decolorization
explanation: >
  The crystal violet–iodine complex is large; during alcohol-acetone
  decolorization the thick, multi-layer peptidoglycan with its
  teichoic-acid crosslinks of gram-positive cells dehydrates and traps
  the complex, so the cells stay purple. In gram-negatives the thin
  peptidoglycan dissolves out of the dye and the outer membrane is
  damaged by the decolorizer, letting the complex escape so the
  colourless cells then stain pink with safranin. The key distractor
  reverses the counterstain logic — safranin is the pink dye that
  gram-positives never get to show.

## Question

The stain is a four-step procedure you should be able to recite in order:
crystal violet, iodine (mordant), decolorize, safranin (counterstain).
Remember the classic exceptions that mislead juniors: mycobacteria need
acid-fast staining, and old cultures or antibiotic-exposed gram-positives
stain gram-variable. Compare the two wall architectures on the
[gram-positive vs gram-negative note](/notes/gram-positive-vs-gram-negative/)
and the [microbiology quiz page](/practice/quiz/microbiology/).
