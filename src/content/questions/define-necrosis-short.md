answer: >
  Necrosis is the spectrum of morphological changes seen in lethally
  injured cells within living tissue, produced by progressive enzymatic
  degradation and protein denaturation after cell death. Its four major
  morphological types are coagulative necrosis, liquefactive necrosis,
  caseous necrosis, and fat necrosis.
explanation: >
  The definition earns marks for two ideas: the cell is dead but the
  tissue is living, and the changes are enzymatic plus denaturation. The
  four types should each carry one example — infarct, abscess,
  tuberculosis, pancreatitis respectively.

## Model answer

Necrosis is always pathological and always follows irreversible cell
injury; the dead cells are digested by their own lysosomal enzymes and by
those of infiltrating leukocytes, while structural proteins denature and
precipitate. Because the cell membrane ruptures, cytoplasmic contents
escape and elicit an acute inflammatory reaction — the feature that most
clearly separates it from apoptosis.

The four major morphological types, each with its classic example:

1. **Coagulative necrosis** — architecture preserved as a ghost outline;
   typical of infarcts in all solid organs except the brain.
2. **Liquefactive necrosis** — tissue digested into liquid, as in brain
   infarction or bacterial abscess.
3. **Caseous necrosis** — amorphous, cheese-like debris with complete
   loss of architecture, enclosed by a granuloma; characteristic of
   tuberculosis.
4. **Fat necrosis** — enzymatic lipolysis of adipocytes with
   saponification, as in acute pancreatitis and traumatic breast injury.

Many texts add fibrinoid necrosis of vessel walls in immune-mediated
injury as a special pattern worth a bonus line. Revise the full spectrum
on the [cell injury note](/notes/cell-injury/).
