answer: Peripherally, often in a horseshoe or ring at the cell margin
explanation: >
  Langhans giant cells, formed by fusion of epithelioid macrophages, place
  their nuclei in a peripheral horseshoe or ring, and are classically seen
  in tuberculosis and sarcoidosis. The key distractor, randomly scattered
  nuclei, describes foreign-body giant cells, which form around
  indigestible material such as sutures or silica. Both are macrophage
  fusion products; only the nuclear arrangement separates them on routine
  stains.

## Question

Do not over-read the distinction: the nuclear pattern is a morphological
clue, not proof of aetiology, and foreign-body giant cells can appear at
the edges of tuberculous granulomas. The higher-yield fact beneath this
question is that the granuloma itself is a type IV hypersensitivity
reaction built from epithelioid macrophages, activated T lymphocytes and
these giant cells. More on the [cell injury note](/notes/cell-injury/).
