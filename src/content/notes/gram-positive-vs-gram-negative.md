  - question: Why do gram-positive bacteria retain the crystal violet stain?
    answer: >
      The crystal violet-iodine complex is large, and the thick,
      multi-layered peptidoglycan of gram-positive cells dehydrates and
      shrinks during alcohol decolorization, trapping the complex inside.
      Gram-negative walls are thin and their outer membrane dissolves in the

  - question: Which component of the gram-negative cell wall causes septic shock?
    answer: >
      Lipopolysaccharide — specifically its lipid A moiety. It binds TLR4 on
      monocytes and macrophages, releasing tumor necrosis factor and
      interleukin-1, which drive fever, vasodilation, capillary leak and
      refractory hypotension. Gram-positive bacteria lack LPS, though their
      teichoic acids can trigger a milder, TLR2-mediated response.
  - question: Why is vancomycin ineffective against gram-negative bacteria?
    answer: >

      against gram-positive organisms — which is exactly why gram-positive
      resistance to it, as in VRE, is such a problem.

## Introduction

The Gram stain is the oldest and still the fastest bench test in
bacteriology: a smear from a colony or a specimen, four reagents, and within
minutes you know which of two cell wall architectures you are facing — which
shortens the antibiotic list before any culture grows. These notes cover the
stain, the walls that explain it, the genera that matter, and the resistance
story that continues in the [antimicrobial resistance topic
hub](/topics/antimicrobial-resistance/).

## The Gram Stain and Why It Works

Four steps, and the chemistry is the exam answer:

1. **Crystal violet** — the primary stain; all cells take it up.
2. **Gram's iodine** — a mordant that forms the large crystal violet-iodine
   complex inside the cell.
3. **Alcohol-acetone decolorizer** — the decisive step. In gram-positive
   cells the thick peptidoglycan dehydrates, its pores shrink, and the
   complex is trapped. In gram-negative cells the thin wall plus the
   dissolving outer membrane lets the complex wash out.
4. **Safranin** — the pink counterstain that colors the now-colorless
   gram-negative cells.

**Gram-positive stain purple; gram-negative stain pink.** The staining
difference is wall architecture, not membrane chemistry of the dye itself —
a distinction examiners like to press.

## Cell Wall Architecture

| Structure | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
|---|---|---|
| Peptidoglycan | Thick, many layers (about 50 percent of wall) | Thin, one to a few layers (5–10 percent) |
| Teichoic acids | Present — major surface antigen | Absent |
| Outer membrane | Absent | Present, with porins |
| Lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin) | Absent | Present — lipid A plus O antigen |
| Periplasmic space | Narrow or absent | Prominent, contains beta-lactamases |
| Lipoprotein | Absent | Present, anchoring outer membrane to peptidoglycan |
| Toxins of note | Exotoxins (proteins, mostly) | Endotoxin, plus exotoxins in some species |

## Clinically Important Genera

**Gram-positive cocci:**

- *Staphylococcus* — clusters, catalase positive. S. aureus (coagulase
  positive: abscesses, endotoxin-negative toxic shock, food poisoning);
  coagulase-negative Staphylococcus epidermidis (line and prosthetic
  infections) and S. saprophyticus (urinary tract infection in young women).
- *Streptococcus* — chains, catalase negative: S. pyogenes (group A —
  pharyngitis, rheumatic fever, nephritis), S. agalactiae (group B —
  neonatal sepsis), S. pneumoniae (pneumonia, otitis, meningitis),
  viridans group (endocarditis), Enterococcus (see VRE below).
- *Enterococcus faecalis* and *E. faecium* — intrinsic resistance to many
  drugs, gut and hospital flora.

**Gram-positive bacilli:** *Bacillus* (anthrax), *Clostridium* (tetanus,
botulism, gas gangrene, pseudomembranous colitis), *Corynebacterium
diphtheriae*, *Listeria monocytogenes* — with *Nocardia* branching and
partially acid-fast.

**Gram-negative cocci:** *Neisseria meningitidis* and *N. gonorrhoeae* —
the diplococci.

**Gram-negative bacilli:** the Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella,
Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus, Enterobacter, Serratia) — glucose
fermenters; plus the non-fermenters *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (opportunistic,
burns, cystic fibrosis, intrinsically resistant) and *Acinetobacter*; the
fastidious coccobacilli *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Bordetella pertussis*;
the curved and spiral *Vibrio cholerae*, *Campylobacter*, *Helicobacter
pylori*; the zoonoses *Brucella*, *Francisella*, *Yersinia pestis*; and the
anaerobe *Bacteroides fragilis*.

**Organisms that dodge the Gram stain:** *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and
leprae (acid-fast by Ziehl-Neelsen — mycolic acids), *Mycoplasma* (no cell
wall), *Treponema pallidum* and *Leptospira* (too thin to see by light
microscopy — darkfield or serology), *Chlamydia* and *Rickettsia*
(intracellular, stain poorly).

## Treatment Implications

The wall decides which drugs can even arrive at their targets:

- **Beta-lactams** (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) block
  peptidoglycan cross-linking and work on both groups — but gram-negative
  enzymes matter. *Staphylococcus aureus* penicillinase means antistaph
  penicillins (flucloxacillin, nafcillin); Enterobacteriaceae produce TEM
  and SHV beta-lactamases in the periplasm, and their ESBL and
  carbapenemase-expressing descendants define the [antimicrobial resistance
  topic hub](/topics/antimicrobial-resistance/).
- **Vancomycin** — binds the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus but is too bulky for
  gram-negative porins: gram-positive only.
- **Aminoglycosides and tetracyclines** — entry routes differ; gram-negative
  uptake of aminoglycosides depends on oxygen-dependent transport.
- **Polymyxins** (colistin) — act on the gram-negative outer membrane,
  damaging LPS; essentially gram-negative only, and a last resort against
  carbapenem-resistant strains.
- **Endotoxin release on therapy** — rapid killing of gram-negative bacteria
  can release LPS and transiently worsen the inflammatory picture; clinically
  managed supportively rather than by drug choice.

## MRSA and VRE

- **MRSA** — *S. aureus* with mecA-encoded PBP2a (also called
  PBP2'), which has low affinity for all conventional beta-lactams.
  Healthcare-associated strains are often multidrug resistant; community
  strains often retain susceptibility to clindamycin and
  trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Definitive therapy: intravenous vancomycin
  (with trough monitoring), daptomycin, linezolid, or ceftaroline, which
  uniquely binds PBP2a.
- **VRE** — enterococci, overwhelmingly *E. faecium*, in which van genes
  (vanA the classic) replace the D-Ala-D-Ala peptide terminus with
  D-Ala-D-Lac, removing vancomycin's docking site. Linezolid and daptomycin
  are the usual agents.
- The cruel irony: vancomycin resistance moving from enterococci into
  *Staphylococcus aureus* has produced rare VRSA strains — the reason
  stewardship is a patient-safety issue, not paperwork.

### Worked exam framing

A vignette: * pus from an abscess shows gram-positive cocci in clusters,
catalase positive, coagulase positive; the patient has a history of
repeated hospital admissions.* That is *S. aureus*, and the admission
history flags MRSA risk — empirical flucloxacillin is wrong; vancomycin or
another MRSA-active agent is needed until susceptibilities return. Change
"clusters" to "chains, catalase negative" and think *Streptococcus*; change
the smear to "gram-negative diplococci" in cerebrospinal fluid and think
*N. meningitidis* with ceftriaxone immediately — the stain reading drives
the first dose.
