## Introduction

Asphyxia deaths are the heart of forensic practice because the question is
rarely *that* the person died of oxygen lack — it is *how*, and whether the
manner was suicide, homicide or accident. The discipline lies in reading the
neck, the lungs and the scene, and in resisting over-interpretation of the
classical signs. The order of the exam answer is fixed: definition,
classification, general signs, then the specific signs of each type.

## Definition and Mechanism

In forensic usage, **asphyxia** is interference with respiration — the
exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide — by any mechanism that prevents
breathing. Mechanistically, deaths classified as asphyxial involve:

- Obstruction of the airway (smothering, choking, hanging, strangulation).
- Compression of the neck vessels and airway.
- Restriction of chest movement (traumatic or positional asphyxia).
- Replacement of breathable air (drowning, confined-space gases).
- Inhibition of cellular oxygen use (classically described for cyanide,
  though handled under toxicology).

## Classification

**Mechanical asphyxia by a ligature or the hands:**

- **Hanging** — suspension of the body by a ligature around the neck; the
  weight of the body provides the force.
- **Ligature strangulation** — the ligature is tightened by a force other
  than body weight.
- **Manual strangulation** — throttling by the hands.
- Strangulation by other means (mugging — forearm compression of the neck;
  the carotid sleeper hold; garrotting).

**Suffocation — obstruction of air entry:**

- **Smothering** — occlusion of mouth and nose (hand, pillow, plastic bag).
- **Choking** — obstruction within the airway by a foreign body, including
  gagging.
- **Traumatic (compression) asphyxia** — crushing of the chest in crowds or
  under weights, with intense cyanosis and petechiae above the compression
  line.
- **Positional asphyxia** — body position preventing breathing, typically in
  intoxication and restraint.
- **Confined spaces and vitiated atmospheres** — suffocating gases.

**Drowning** — submersion in a fluid, a distinct chapter because death
results from fluid in the airways and the biochemical consequences.

## General Signs of Asphyxia

The classical picture is:

1. Cyanosis of the face, lips and nail beds.
2. **Petechial haemorrhages** in the conjunctivae, face, and as **Tardieu
   spots** over the lungs, heart and other viscera.
3. Congestion and oedema of the face and neck organs.
4. Fluidity of the blood (though common post-mortem, it is emphasized in the
   classical texts).
5. Engorgement of the right heart and great veins.

Examiners reward the caveat: these signs are **general and non-specific**.
They reflect raised venous pressure and hypoxia of many causes, including
natural deaths, and they cannot by themselves establish asphyxia or its
type.

## Hanging and Strangulation: The Distinguishing Table

| Feature | Hanging | Ligature strangulation | Manual strangulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction of mark | Oblique, upward toward suspension point | Horizontal | No groove; fingertip bruises |
| Depth | Deepest opposite the knot | Roughly uniform | — |
| Continuity | Interrupted at the nape | Usually complete circle | — |
| Level on neck | Above the thyroid cartilage, often at the angle of the jaw | At or below the thyroid cartilage | Anywhere on the front and sides of the neck |
| Facial appearance | Usually pale if fully suspended | Intense congestion and cyanosis | Congestion, petechiae |
| Neck injuries | Possible fracture of the hyoid or thyroid cornu (typically the greater horn, in a minority), haemorrhage into sternomastoid and neck muscles | More frequent hyoid and cartilage fractures, muscle haemorrhage | Muscle haemorrhage, sometimes fracture of hyoid; nail marks |
| Manner | Suicidal (the great majority) | Homicidal (occasionally suicidal) | Virtually always homicidal |

In **typical hanging** the knot lies at the nape and the force is symmetric;
in **atypical hanging** the knot is elsewhere on the neck and the groove is
deeper opposite it. Judicial hanging — a long drop producing cervical spine
and cord injury — is a separate entity. A suspension groove without vital
reaction (no redness, blistering or hardening) suggests the body was
suspended after death: the key finding in staged suicides.

## Suffocation and Drowning

- **Smothering** — often no external signs; look for abrasions or bruising
  around the mouth and nose, and for injuries to the inner lips against the
  teeth if a hand was clamped. In infancy, unexplained suffocation is a
  diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Choking** — the foreign body may be found at autopsy; pay attention to
  the history of a sudden collapse while eating (the café coronary).
- **Traumatic asphyxia** — intense violaceous congestion and petechiae
  confined above the level of chest compression.
- **Drowning** — the specific findings:
  - **Fine, white, copious froth** at the mouth and nostrils, often
    persistent and increasing under pressure.
  - **Emphysema aquosum** — large, ballooned, doughy lungs that pit under
    the finger and crepitate; cut surfaces exude frothy fluid.
  - **Washerwoman's hands and feet** from immersion; water in the stomach
    supports live entry but is non-specific.
  - **Cadaveric spasm** with grass, weeds or mud clutched in the hands is
    powerful evidence the victim entered the water alive.
  - **Diatom test** — recovering diatoms from bone marrow, liver or kidney
    and matching them to the water body supports drowning; contamination and
    laboratory technique limit its weight.
  - Middle-ear and mastoid haemorrhages and biochemical changes in blood
    from the two sides of the heart are supportive, historical or
    contested, and are best cited as adjuncts rather than proof.

### Worked exam framing

A long-question framing worth rehearsing: *a body is found suspended by a
sari from a ceiling fan; describe your autopsy protocol.* The structured
answer: examine the scene and the suspension point; note body weight versus
ligature strength; photograph the neck before removing the ligature; describe
the groove — direction, depth, continuity, level, vital reaction; dissect
the neck layer by layer after draining the blood (to avoid artefact); look
for hyoid and thyroid cartilage injury and muscle haemorrhage; open the
airway and vessels; then interpret. The groove's obliquity and the presence
of vital reaction decide the direction of the case — hanging while alive, or
suspension of a dead body.
