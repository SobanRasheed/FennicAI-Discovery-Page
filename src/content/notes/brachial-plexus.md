## Introduction

The brachial plexus is the nerve supply of the entire upper limb. It looks
intimidating drawn as a diagram, but its logic is simple: five roots combine
into trunks, trunks divide, divisions recombine into cords, and cords give
the named nerves you test at the bedside. Once you can reproduce that
sequence, every classic injury — Erb's palsy, Klumpke's palsy, wrist drop —
becomes a predictable consequence of where along the plexus the damage sits.
For more on how nerve lesions produce the pain patterns students confuse at
the bedside, see the [pain pathways topic hub](/topics/pain-pathways/).

## Formation: Roots to Cords

The plexus is formed by the **anterior primary rami of C5, C6, C7, C8 and
T1**. (A contribution from C4 makes it "prefixed"; from T2, "postfixed" —
asked as a one-liner MCQ.)

- **Roots** C5–T1 unite into **trunks**: C5+C6 form the superior trunk, C7
  continues as the middle trunk, and C8+T1 form the inferior trunk.
- Each trunk divides into **anterior and posterior divisions** — six in total.
- The divisions recombine into **cords** around the axillary artery, named
  for their position relative to its second part: anterior divisions of
  superior and middle trunks form the **lateral cord**; all three posterior
  divisions form the **posterior cord**; the anterior division of the inferior
  trunk continues as the **medial cord**.

Note the exam favourite: the posterior cord receives fibres from **all five
roots**, which is why every root value in the plexus contributes to extensor
muscles.

## Branches Worth Memorizing

Root and trunk branches are small but clinically famous:

- **Dorsal scapular nerve** (C5) — rhomboids.
- **Long thoracic nerve** (C5–C7) — serratus anterior; injury causes winged
  scapula.
- **Suprascapular nerve** (C5–C6, from the superior trunk) — supraspinatus
  and infraspinatus.

The cords give the terminal branches:

| Cord | Branches | Root value |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral | Lateral pectoral nerve; musculocutaneous nerve; lateral root of median nerve | C5–C7 |
| Posterior | Upper and lower subscapular nerves; thoracodorsal nerve; axillary nerve; radial nerve | C5–T1 |
| Medial | Medial pectoral nerve; medial cutaneous nerves of arm and forearm; ulnar nerve; medial root of median nerve | C8–T1 (ulnar may carry C7) |

The median nerve is formed by lateral (C5–C7) and medial (C8–T1) roots
joining on the front of the third part of the axillary artery — the two cords
"shake hands" over it.

## Injuries: The Table Examiners Ask For

| Injury | Site / roots | Motor loss | Deformity or sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Erb's palsy | Upper trunk C5–C6 | Deltoid, biceps, supraspinatus, wrist extensors | Waiter's tip: adducted, internally rotated arm, extended elbow, pronated forearm, flexed wrist |
| Klumpke's palsy | Lower trunk C8–T1 | Intrinsic hand muscles, long flexors | Claw hand; ± Horner syndrome |
| Radial nerve at spiral groove | Radial nerve, mid-shaft humerus | Wrist and finger extensors (triceps spared) | Wrist drop; sensory loss posterior forearm, dorsum of first web space |
| Radial nerve in axilla | Radial nerve, axilla | All radial muscles including triceps | Wrist drop + loss of elbow extension (crutch pressure) |
| Axillary nerve at surgical neck | Axillary nerve | Deltoid, teres minor | Loss of shoulder abduction from 15–90°; numb regimental badge area |
| Long thoracic nerve | C5–C7 on chest wall | Serratus anterior | Winged scapula |

### Worked exam framing

A vignette asks: *a newborn after shoulder dystocia holds the arm adducted
and internally rotated with a flexed wrist — where is the lesion?* That is
the waiter's tip posture, so the answer is the **upper trunk (C5–C6), Erb's
palsy**. Change the vignette to a fall on the outstretched hand with
inability to abduct beyond 15° and numbness over the deltoid, and the answer
becomes **axillary nerve at the surgical neck of the humerus**. Examiners
rarely leave the table above — they only re-dress it.

## Clinical Correlations

- **Birth injury:** excessive lateral flexion of the neck during delivery
  stretches the upper trunk (shoulder dystocia) or tears the lower trunk
  (breech delivery pulling the arms). Prognosis of Erb's palsy is generally
  better than Klumpke's.
- **Thoracic outlet syndrome:** a cervical rib or tight scalene band
  compresses the inferior trunk (C8–T1) plus the subclavian artery — pain,
  numbness along the ulnar border, and a positive Adson's test.
- **Saturday night palsy:** pressure on the arm against a bench compresses
  the radial nerve in the spiral groove — the classic cause of painless
  wrist drop.
- **Post-mastectomy weakness:** injury to the long thoracic nerve during
  breast surgery is the standard clinical source of winged scapula.

fact. Then test yourself the forward way (name the branches of the posterior
