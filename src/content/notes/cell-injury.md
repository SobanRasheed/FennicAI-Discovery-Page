  - question: Which features distinguish reversible from irreversible cell injury?
    answer: >
      Reversible injury shows cellular swelling, loss of microvilli, and
      fatty change, with clumped chromatin but an intact plasma membrane.
      Irreversibility is marked by extensive membrane damage, mitochondrial
      vacuolization, lysosomal rupture and nuclear changes — pyknosis,
      karyorrhexis, karyolysis. Functionally, the cell can no longer
      generate ATP or maintain its permeability barrier.
  - question: Why does the brain undergo liquefactive rather than coagulative necrosis after infarction?
    answer: >
      Brain tissue has little connective tissue stroma to hold a coagulative
      architecture, and it is rich in lysosomal enzymes and lipids. The dead
      tissue therefore digests into a soft, liquid cavity that is eventually
      cleared by macrophages, leaving a glial scar.

      Coagulative — myocardial infarction or renal infarct. Caseous — the
      necrotic centre of a tuberculous granuloma. Fibrinoid — the vessel wall

      nodosa.

## Introduction

Everything in pathology begins here: aetiology of disease, then mechanism,
then morphology. Cell injury is the first chapter of that story. The
examiner's questions are predictable — what caused it, is it reversible, what
does it look like, and which pattern of necrosis is this? The [inflammation
and repair topic hub](/topics/inflammation-and-repair/) picks up exactly
where this note ends, at the tissue's response to death.

## Causes of Cell Injury

- **Hypoxia and ischemia** — the commonest cause: atherosclerotic
  occlusion, thrombus, strangulation of hernia, anemia, carbon monoxide
  poisoning. Ischemia injures more than hypoxia alone because glucose and
  washout are lost too.
- **Physical agents** — trauma, burns, frostbite, radiation, electric
  shock, pressure.
- **Chemical agents and drugs** — from alcohol and heavy metals to
  therapeutic drugs in overdose; even oxygen at high concentration
  (free-radical injury).
- **Infectious agents** — viruses, bacteria including exotoxins and
  endotoxin, fungi, parasites.
- **Immunologic reactions** — anaphylaxis, autoimmune disease, immune-complex
  deposition.
- **Genetic derangements** — from enzyme defects to structural protein
  mutations.
- **Nutritional imbalances** — protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin
  deficiencies, and obesity itself as a pro-inflammatory state.

## Mechanisms: The Pathogenesis That Decides Reversibility

Whatever the cause, injured cells converge on a few final pathways:

1. **ATP depletion** — failure of the sodium pump causes sodium and water
   influx (cellular swelling) and of the calcium pump causes cytosolic
   calcium accumulation.
2. **Mitochondrial damage** — both victim and executioner: damaged
   mitochondria release cytochrome c (triggering apoptosis) and generate
   further free radicals.
3. **Calcium influx** — activates phospholipases, proteases, ATPases and
   endonucleases, which dismantle membranes, cytoskeleton and DNA.
4. **Reactive oxygen species** — superoxide, hydroxyl radical and hydrogen
   peroxide attack lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins and DNA; central to
   ischemia-reperfusion injury, inflammation and chemical toxicity.
5. **Membrane damage** — the critical event. While plasma, mitochondrial and
   lysosomal membranes hold, the injury is reversible; once they fail,
   enzymes leak, digestion begins, and the cell is irreversibly dead.

The **reperfusion paradox** is examinable: restoring flow after ischemia
delivers oxygen that fuels new free radical bursts, and infiltrating
neutrophils add their own — so injury can worsen just as treatment begins.

## Morphology of Injury

**Reversible:**

- *Cellular swelling* (hydropic change, vacuolar degeneration) — pale,
  swollen, heavy organs; microscopically clear cytoplasmic vacuoles.
- *Fatty change* — lipid vacuoles in parenchymal cells; classically in the
  liver with alcohol, anoxia or toxins.
- Other reversible touches: eosinophilia of cytoplasm, chromatin clumping.

**Irreversible — necrosis** shows:

- *Cytoplasmic* — increased eosinophilia, then myelin figures, and
  ultimately calcification of dead cells.
- *Nuclear* — **pyknosis** (condensation), **karyorrhexis**
  (fragmentation), **karyolysis** (dissolution by DNase).
- The **inflammatory response** that follows is what distinguishes necrosis
  histologically from apoptosis.

Clinically, dead cells release their enzymes: this is the basis of serum
**troponin** after myocardial infarction, **ALT/AST** in hepatocyte injury,
and **lipase** in acute pancreatitis — morphology made measurable.

## The Six Patterns of Necrosis

| Type | Mechanism / appearance | Classic example |
|---|---|---|
| Coagulative | Architecture preserved as firm ghost outline; denatured proteins block digestion | Myocardial, renal, splenic infarcts |
| Liquefactive | Enzymatic digestion to liquid | Brain infarct; abscess cavity |
| Caseous | Amorphous granular debris, "cheese-like", ringed by granuloma | Tuberculosis |
| Fat | Lipases release fatty acids that saponify; chalky white flecks | Acute pancreatitis; traumatic breast fat necrosis |
| Fibrinoid | Immune complexes and plasma proteins deposited in vessel walls, bright pink | Polyarteritis nodosa; malignant hypertension |
| Gangrenous | Coagulative necrosis of a limb, plus liquefactive if infected (wet gangrene) | Diabetic foot, strangulated hernia |

### Worked exam framing

A vignette describes a 55-year-old diabetic with a black, dry, mummified
toe: that is **dry gangrene** — coagulative necrosis in a limb. Add foul
smell, crepitus and a wet, putrid appearance, and it becomes **wet
gangrene**, where bacterial liquefactive necrosis is superimposed. The same
slide-based logic applies everywhere in pathology: name the pattern, then
name the disease.

## Necrosis versus Apoptosis

| Feature | Necrosis | Apoptosis |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Injury, always pathologic | Programmed; physiologic or pathologic |
| Cells affected | Contiguous groups | Single, scattered cells |
| Cell size | Swollen | Shrunken |
| Nucleus | Pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis | Condensation and fragmentation into nucleosome-sized bits |
| Membrane | Ruptured | Intact, budding into apoptotic bodies |
| ATP required | No | Yes |
| Inflammation | Present | Absent |
| Examples | Infarct, toxin injury | Embryologic remodelling, thymic involution; viral hepatitis councilman bodies, tumor regression |

The exam trap is **apoptosis in disease**: it is not always physiologic.
Viral hepatitis kills hepatocytes largely by apoptosis (Councilman bodies),
and failure of apoptosis contributes to cancer and autoimmunity — which is
the conceptual bridge to neoplasia later in the course.

## Mediators of Inflammation: A Bridge

Cell death summons inflammation, and the mediators are the next exam's
material. In outline:

- **Histamine** from mast cells — the fastest mediator of vasodilation and
  vascular permeability.
- **Arachidonic acid metabolites** — prostaglandins (vasodilation, pain,
  fever) and leukotrienes (bronchoconstriction, chemotaxis); the target of
  NSAIDs.
- **Complement** — C3a and C5a anaphylatoxins, C5a chemotaxis, membrane
  attack complex.
- **Cytokines** — interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor drive fever and
  the acute phase response.
- **Kinins** — bradykinin, the mediator of pain and permeability.

and repair continues in the [inflammation and repair topic
hub](/topics/inflammation-and-repair/).
