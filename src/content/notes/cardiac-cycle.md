## Introduction

The cardiac cycle is the heart's repeating unit of work: fill, contract,
empty, relax. Physiology exams test it three ways — a table of phases, the
timing of heart sounds, and a calculation of cardiac output or ejection
fraction. All three rest on one principle: **valves open and close passively
in response to pressure gradients**, never by their own effort. Keep that in
mind and every phase becomes deducible rather than memorizable.

## Duration and Divisions

At a heart rate of 75 beats per minute the cycle lasts **0.8 seconds**:
systole about 0.3 s and diastole about 0.5 s. As heart rate rises, diastole
shortens proportionally more than systole — which is why very fast rates
compromise filling and coronary perfusion, since the coronaries fill during
diastole.

## The Seven Phases

| Phase | Atrial pressure vs ventricular | Ventricular pressure | Valves | Ventricular volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Atrial systole | Atrial > ventricular | Rising slightly | AV open, semilunar closed | Rising to EDV (~120 mL) |
| 2. Isovolumetric contraction | Atrial < ventricular | Rising steeply | All closed | Unchanged (EDV) |
| 3. Rapid ejection | Atrial < ventricular | Rising to peak (~120 mmHg) | Semilunar open, AV closed | Falling fast |
| 4. Reduced ejection | Falling | Falling slowly from peak | Semilunar open, AV closed | Falling slowly to ~50 mL |
| 5. Isovolumetric relaxation | Atrial rising, ventricular falling | Falling steeply | All closed | Unchanged (ESV) |
| 6. Rapid filling | Atrial > ventricular | Near zero | AV open, semilunar closed | Rising fast |
| 7. Diastasis (slow filling) | Atrial ≈ ventricular | Near zero | AV open, semilunar closed | Rising slowly |

Two details earn marks: the **ventricle never empties** — about 50 mL
remains as end-systolic volume — and **most filling occurs in phase 6**, not
during atrial systole, which only tops the ventricle up by 20–30 percent
(the atrial kick).

### Reading the pressure traces

The **aortic pressure trace** shows the **dicrotic notch** when the aortic
valve closes, and the aortic pressure never falls to zero because elastic
recoil of the large arteries sustains it. The **left atrial (or venous
pulse) trace** shows the **a, c and v waves**: *a* from atrial contraction,
*c* from bulging of AV valve leaflets during isovolumetric contraction, and
*v* from venous filling of the atrium while the AV valve is shut. The *x
descent* follows atrial relaxation, the *y descent* the opening of the AV
valve.

## Heart Sounds

- **S1** — closure of mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of
  isovolumetric contraction; coincides with the R wave of the ECG.
- **S2** — closure of aortic and pulmonary valves at the start of
  isovolumetric relaxation; its splitting widens on inspiration because
  increased venous return delays pulmonary valve closure.
- **S3** — rapid ventricular filling in early diastole; normal in youth,
  suspicious of failure in older adults.
- **S4** — atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle, just before S1;
  absent in atrial fibrillation because the atria do not contract.

## The Pressure-Volume Loop

Draw volume on the x-axis and pressure on the y-axis for the left ventricle:

1. **Filling** — the bottom limb moving right from ESV (~50 mL) to EDV
   (~120 mL) at near-zero pressure.
2. **Isovolumetric contraction** — the vertical rise from EDV at constant
   volume.
3. **Ejection** — the top curving limb from the aortic valve opening
   pressure to peak systolic pressure (~120 mmHg), then back down to ESV.
4. **Isovolumetric relaxation** — the vertical drop from ESV to the filling
   line.

The width of the loop is the stroke volume; the area inside the loop is the
external stroke work. Aortic stenosis makes the loop taller and narrower;
volume overload makes it wider; heart failure shifts it rightward.

## Calculations: The Worked Example Exams Use

A patient has an end-diastolic volume of 120 mL, an end-systolic volume of
50 mL, and a heart rate of 72 beats per minute.

- **Stroke volume** = EDV − ESV = 120 − 50 = 70 mL.
- **Cardiac output** = SV × HR = 70 × 72 = 5040 mL/min ≈ 5 L/min.
- **Ejection fraction** = 70 ÷ 120 × 100 ≈ 58 percent.

Now the exam twist: the same patient develops a cardiomyopathy, EDV rises to
200 mL and stroke volume falls to 60 mL — ejection fraction drops to 30
percent, defining significant systolic dysfunction even though stroke volume
has fallen only modestly. This is exactly why ejection fraction, not stroke
volume, is the number cardiologists quote.

## Clinical Framing

A short-case viva will point at the phase table and ask "which murmur occurs
here?" Systolic murmurs occupy the interval between S1 and S2 (ejection
phases), diastolic murmurs the interval after S2 (filling phases). A patient
in atrial fibrillation loses the atrial kick and the *a* wave; if their
ventricle is also stiff, filling suffers twice — the physiological reason
such patients deteriorate when the rate runs fast and diastasis shortens.
