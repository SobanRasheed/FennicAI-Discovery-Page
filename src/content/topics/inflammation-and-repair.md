  - question: What is the difference between acute and chronic inflammation?
    answer: >
      Acute inflammation lasts minutes to days, shows vasodilation,
      edema, and neutrophil infiltration, and often resolves fully.
      Chronic inflammation lasts weeks to years, is dominated by
      macrophages and lymphocytes, and tends to end in fibrosis rather
      than full restoration.
  - question: Which mediators cause the cardinal signs?
    answer: >
      Histamine and bradykinin cause vasodilation and vessel leakiness,
      producing redness, heat, and swelling. Prostaglandins amplify pain
      and fever. Complement fragments and cytokines such as IL-1 and TNF
      recruit and activate leukocytes.
  - question: When does tissue regenerate instead of scarring?
    answer: >
      Labile cells such as surface epithelia divide throughout life and
      regenerate readily. Stable cells such as liver can divide when
      triggered. Permanent cells, cardiac myocytes and neurons, do not
      divide meaningfully, so their death always heals by scarring.
  - question: What is granulation tissue?
    answer: >
      It is the scaffolding of healing: proliferating small blood vessels
      and fibroblasts in a loose extracellular matrix. Type III collagen
      is laid down first and later remodeled toward type I, which is why
      young scars are weak and mature scars are strong.

## What it is

Inflammation is the vascular and cellular response to injury, designed to
deliver leukocytes and plasma proteins to the damaged site, dispose of
the cause, and set the stage for healing. Acute inflammation is
stereotyped: brief vasoconstriction, then vasodilation and increased
permeability, then margination and emigration of neutrophils. Mediators
including histamine, bradykinin, complement, prostaglandins, and the
cytokines IL-1 and TNF coordinate each step. Chronic inflammation, by
contrast, is a mononuclear infiltrate of macrophages and lymphocytes,
sustained by a persisting cause such as a foreign body, an immune
reaction, or an intracellular microbe. Healing then takes one of two
roads: regeneration of like-for-like parenchyma, or repair by connective
tissue, producing granulation tissue and, ultimately, a collagen scar.

## Why it spans subjects

In physiology, the vascular changes explain the cardinal signs, rubor,
calor, tumor, dolor, and functio laesa, as taught in
[physiology](/subjects/physiology/). In biochemistry, the mediators are
arachidonic acid metabolism in action: cyclooxygenase yields
prostaglandins and thromboxane, lipoxygenase yields leukotrienes, which
is also exactly where NSAIDs act, a bridge to
[biochemistry](/subjects/biochemistry/). In pathology, inflammation is the
direct sequel to the processes in the
[cell injury note](/notes/cell-injury/): cells that cannot adapt die, and
the inflammatory response decides whether the outcome is resolution,
abscess, or fibrosis. The same mediator cascade interacts with clotting,
connecting this hub to [hemostasis](/topics/hemostasis/), and infection
remains its most examined trigger, connecting it to
[antimicrobial resistance](/topics/antimicrobial-resistance/).
