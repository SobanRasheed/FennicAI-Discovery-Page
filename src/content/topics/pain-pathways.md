  Pain works like a building's fire alarm system. Nociceptors, the free
  nerve endings in your skin and organs, are smoke detectors tuned to
  damage rather than smoke. When tissue is injured, chemicals released at
  the site set the detector off. The alarm signal travels along two kinds
  of wiring: thin fast cables that carry sharp, sudden pain, and slower
  cables that carry the dull ache that follows.

  The signal crosses to the other side of the spinal cord almost
  immediately and runs up to a relay station in the thalamus, which
  forwards it to the brain for conscious perception. There is also a
  volume knob: signals from large touch fibers and from the brain itself
  can turn the alarm down at the spinal level, which is why rubbing a
  bumped elbow helps. Painkillers act at different points, some muffling
  the detector, others turning down the volume knob.

faqs:
  - question: Which fibers carry pain?
    answer: >
      A-delta fibers are thinly myelinated and carry fast, sharp, well
      localized pain. C fibers are unmyelinated and carry slow, dull,
      poorly localized pain. Both are small-diameter fibers, which is the
      anatomical basis of gate control theory.
  - question: Where does the spinothalamic tract cross over?
    answer: >
      Second-order neurons in the dorsal horn decussate in the anterior
      white commissure within one or two spinal segments, then ascend
      contralaterally in the anterolateral column. This is why
      cord hemisection causes contralateral loss of pain and temperature
      below the lesion.
  - question: What does gate control theory actually claim?
    answer: >
      Melzack and Wall proposed that substantia gelatinosa cells in the
      dorsal horn act as a gate. Large-diameter touch fiber activity
      closes the gate and suppresses pain transmission, while small-fiber
      activity opens it. Descending brain signals also bias the gate,
      explaining analgesia under stress and attention.
  - question: Where do the main analgesics act in this pathway?
    answer: >
      NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated sensitization of nociceptors
      at transduction, local anesthetics block conduction in the nerve,
      and opioids act on receptors in the dorsal horn and periaqueductal
      gray to activate descending inhibition and damp transmission.

## What it is

Pain is a four-step process: transduction, transmission, perception, and
modulation. Transduction begins when inflammatory chemicals, bradykinin,
prostaglandins, substance P, histamine, sensitize high-threshold
nociceptors. Transmission carries the signal on A-delta and C fibers to
the dorsal horn, where second-order neurons decussate in the anterior
white commissure and ascend as the contralateral lateral spinothalamic
tract to the ventral posterolateral nucleus of the thalamus, and on to
the cortex. Modulation adjusts the volume: gate control, where large
mechanoreceptor input suppresses small-fiber transmission in the
substantia gelatinosa, and descending pathways from the periaqueductal
gray through the nucleus raphe magnus, using serotonin and endogenous
opioids. Each analgesic class targets a named step, which is what makes
this topic examinable from both directions.

## Why it spans subjects

In anatomy, the spinothalamic tract is core neuroanatomy, and peripheral
nerve injury explains neuropathic pain, as seen with lesions of large
plexuses such as the [brachial plexus](/notes/brachial-plexus/). In
physiology, sensory receptors, adaptation, and the gate and descending
systems are standard fare across
[physiology](/subjects/physiology/). In biochemistry, the sensitizing
mediators are eicosanoids derived from arachidonic acid, and
prostaglandin synthesis is precisely the pathway COX inhibitors block,
bridging to [biochemistry](/subjects/biochemistry/) and to the mediator
cascade behind [inflammation](/topics/inflammation-and-repair/).
Ischemic pain, the angina of a thrombosed vessel, ties the topic to
[hemostasis](/topics/hemostasis/), and the pharmacology of analgesics is
examinable alongside the [autonomic pharmacology
note](/notes/autonomic-pharmacology/), which shares many of the same
receptor systems.

spinothalamic tract in physiology and neuroanatomy, often as clinical
vignettes of cord lesions. Second Professional pharmacology asks where
NSAIDs and opioids act in the pathway and how adjuvants such as
amitriptyline work in neuropathic pain. Practice both angles with the
