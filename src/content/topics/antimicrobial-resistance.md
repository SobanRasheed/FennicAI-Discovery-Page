  - question: What does MRSA mean mechanically?
    answer: >
      Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus carries the mecA gene,
      which encodes an altered penicillin-binding protein called PBP2a.
      Beta-lactams cannot bind it well, so the cell wall still gets built.
      Treatment typically falls to vancomycin or linezolid.
  - question: How does VRE resist vancomycin?
    answer: >
      Enterococcus with the vanA gene cluster replaces the terminal
      D-alanine-D-alanine in its cell wall precursors with
      D-alanine-D-lactate. Vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Ala, so the substitution
      removes its target.
  - question: What are ESBL organisms?
    answer: >
      Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases are plasmid-borne enzymes, mostly
      in E. coli and Klebsiella, that hydrolyze penicillins and
      third-generation cephalosporins. They are treated with
      carbapenems, which is why lab reporting of ESBL matters so much.
  - question: What is antimicrobial stewardship?
    answer: >
      The disciplined use of antibiotics: correct indication, correct drug
      guided by culture, correct dose and route, timely de-escalation, and
      the shortest effective duration. Its goal is to cure the patient
      today without breeding resistance for tomorrow.

## What it is

Antimicrobial resistance is the ability of microbes to survive
concentrations of drugs designed to kill or inhibit them. Bacterial
resistance rests on four core mechanisms. Enzymatic inactivation destroys
the drug, as beta-lactamases and ESBLs do to penicillins and
cephalosporins. Altered targets change the drug's binding site, as in the
mecA-encoded PBP2a of MRSA or the D-Ala-D-Lac substitution of VRE. Efflux
pumps actively export drugs such as tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones.
Reduced permeability, typically porin loss in gram-negatives, keeps the
drug out entirely. Genes for these mechanisms spread vertically to
offspring and horizontally through plasmids and transposons, which is how
resistance races ahead of new drug development.

## Why it spans subjects

In microbiology, structure predicts mechanism: the outer membrane and
porins of gram-negatives explain why vancomycin cannot work on them, a
point made in the [gram-positive versus gram-negative
note](/notes/gram-positive-vs-gram-negative/). In biochemistry, every
mechanism above is enzyme and receptor chemistry, competitive inhibition
written in bacteria, as covered across [biochemistry](/subjects/biochemistry/).
In pharmacology, resistance dictates prescribing: dose optimization,
combination therapy, and de-escalation are the antimicrobial chapter
applied, so drill the [pharmacology quiz](/practice/quiz/pharmacology/).
Resistance also feeds back into patient care through infection and
sepsis, the severe end of
[inflammation](/topics/inflammation-and-repair/), and through
culture-and-sensitivity reporting, a direct application of
[diagnostic test accuracy](/topics/diagnostic-test-accuracy/).

## Where it's examined

Second Professional MBBS examines resistance mechanisms in microbiology
(general bacteriology, antibiotic sensitivity patterns) and the
antimicrobial agents chapter of pharmacology, often as one-best-answer
questions giving a lab report and asking for the drug of choice.
Community medicine revisits stewardship and surveillance in hospital
infection control. Build fluency with the
[microbiology quiz](/practice/quiz/microbiology/).
